\begin{abstract}
Large pretrained models such as large language models (LLMs) provide strong
prior knowledge that can accelerate sequential decision making, yet combining
such frozen predictors with adaptive contextual bandit learners remains
poorly understood from a regret-theoretic standpoint.
Existing model-selection results guarantee performance competitive with the
best base algorithm in hindsight but fail to explain why combining a fixed
policy with an adaptive learner can \emph{strictly outperform both}---a
phenomenon recently observed empirically and termed \emph{reward
multiplication}.
In this work, we develop a regret-based explanation for this effect in linear
contextual bandits. We show that a fixed policy should be interpreted not
merely as a competitor, but as a source of structured exploration that
reshapes the geometry of the LinUCB design matrix.
Concretely, we prove that under mild coverage assumptions the cumulative
regret of a confidence-gated meta-algorithm decomposes into (i) a small
additive early-reward term from the frozen policy, plus (ii) an adaptive term
whose uncertainty radius is multiplicatively reduced by a factor
$\sqrt{\lambda/(\lambda+\nu\tau)}$ relative to cold-start LinUCB.
We further introduce a deterministic confidence-gated selector that routes
decisions between one or more frozen experts and an online LinUCB learner
based on self-normalized uncertainty scores, and translate the regret
decomposition into an average-reward multiplication bound against the best
frozen policy. Experiments on a large-scale entity-linking task with
Flan-T5 predictors of varying capacity empirically validate the theory:
our meta-algorithm achieves strict reward multiplication precisely when the
adaptive learner's asymptotic ceiling exceeds the frozen policy's accuracy,
and fails gracefully outside this regime.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sequential decision making studies how an agent can adapt to an unknown
environment through repeated interaction, where it observes contexts,
selects actions, and receives feedback over time
\cite{zhu2022largeaction,zhu2022contextual}.
This framework underlies a wide range of applications, including
recommendation systems \cite{li2010contextual,agarwal2016making},
healthcare decision support \cite{tewari2017ads,svensson2023sequential},
and dialogue systems \cite{li2016deep}.
In many modern applications, large pretrained models such as large language
models (LLMs) provide strong prior knowledge that can guide early decisions,
raising the question of how such fixed predictors should be integrated into
adaptive sequential learning algorithms
\cite{brown2020language,ouyang2022training,achiam2023gpt,
alamdari2024jumpstarting,abbasiyadkori2024believe}.
Recent work increasingly highlights the complementary strengths of bandit
algorithms and foundation models, where bandits provide principled
exploration guarantees while pretrained models contribute structured prior
information \cite{bouneffouf2025banditsllms}.

Existing efforts to incorporate LLMs into sequential decision making largely
follow two directions.
The first treats decision making itself as a sequence modeling problem and
adapts transformer-based architectures through pretraining or finetuning to
interactive environments
\cite{chen2021decision,zheng2022online,reid2022can,
sun2023smart,raparthy2023generalization,lee2023supervised}.
While effective empirically, these approaches require substantial
computational resources.
The second direction relies on prompt engineering and in-context learning to
deploy pretrained models directly as policies
\cite{krishnamurthy2024can}.
Such approaches are supported by recent evidence that LLMs can exhibit
in-context reinforcement learning behavior, enabling sequential adaptation
without gradient-based updates \cite{monea2024llms}.
Although computationally attractive, these methods typically require
frontier-scale models and carefully designed prompts to achieve reliable
performance.

An alternative approach, introduced in \cite{chen2024llmdecision}, combines a
fixed pretrained policy with an adaptive contextual bandit learner using an
online model selection framework
\cite{auer2002nonstochastic,agarwal2017corralling,pacchiano2020model,
foster2020beyond}.
This method is motivated by a simple empirical observation: pretrained
policies often achieve strong initial performance due to prior knowledge,
while adaptive bandit algorithms improve gradually through exploration.
Recent studies further demonstrate that LLM-derived priors can significantly
accelerate early learning by improving exploration efficiency in contextual
bandits \cite{alamdari2024jumpstarting}.
Surprisingly, combining the two can yield performance substantially higher
than either component alone over finite horizons, a phenomenon referred to as
\emph{reward multiplication}.

While existing model selection guarantees ensure performance competitive
with the best base learner in hindsight, they do not explain why combining a
fixed policy with an adaptive learner can strictly outperform both.
In particular, standard expert-aggregation analyses treat the fixed policy as
a competitor rather than as a mechanism that reshapes the learning dynamics
of the adaptive algorithm.

In this work, we develop a regret-theoretic explanation for this phenomenon.
We show that a fixed policy should be viewed not merely as a policy to
imitate, but as a source of structured exploration that alters the geometry
of the design matrix in linear contextual bandits.
When early actions selected by the fixed policy align with
reward-relevant directions, they accelerate the growth of the covariance
matrix in informative subspaces, shrink confidence ellipsoids more rapidly,
and improve short-horizon performance.
This produces both an additive early reward gain and a multiplicative
reduction in the uncertainty of the adaptive learner.

Motivated by this perspective, we study the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item When can a fixed policy improve the average reward of an adaptive
    contextual bandit algorithm, especially over finite horizons?
    \item If multiple fixed policies are available (e.g., different prompts,
    embeddings, or pretrained predictors), how should they be combined with
    online exploration?
\end{enumerate}

To address the second question, we propose a confidence-gated meta-algorithm
that dynamically routes decisions between candidate fixed policies and an
adaptive contextual bandit learner.
The method evaluates the uncertainty of each estimator relative to the
current context and selects the policy with the smallest confidence radius.
As the adaptive learner gathers information and its confidence ellipsoid
contracts, control naturally shifts toward the online algorithm, yielding
strong short-horizon performance while preserving optimal asymptotic regret.

\paragraph{Our Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{A geometric mechanism for reward multiplication.}
    We give a concrete linear-bandit construction
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}) in which, for $d\ge T$, a cold-start
    LinUCB provably plays a suboptimal action on every round while an
    anisotropically warm-started LinUCB plays the optimal action at every
    round with high probability.
    This isolates the mechanism by which a fixed policy alters LinUCB's
    exploration trajectory.
    \item \textbf{A confidence-gated meta-algorithm for multiple frozen
    policies} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}).
    Given $m$ frozen estimators $\{(\widehat\theta_i,V^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m$ and
    an online LinUCB learner, the algorithm routes each round to the
    predictor with the smallest self-normalized uncertainty, and admits an
    optional one-way handover criterion that retires the frozen experts
    once LinUCB's lower confidence bound dominates their upper bounds.
    \item \textbf{A warm-started regret decomposition.}
    Under coverage assumptions on the frozen expert's early actions, we
    prove (Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}) that the meta-algorithm's
    regret decomposes as $\tau\varepsilon_0 + \mathcal{O}(\beta_T\sqrt{r(T-\tau)\log(1+L^2(T-\tau)/(\lambda+\nu\tau))})$,
    whose adaptive term is smaller than cold-start LinUCB's by a
    multiplicative factor $\Psi(\tau,\nu)\approx\sqrt{\lambda/(\lambda+\nu\tau)}$.
    \item \textbf{Average-reward multiplication over the best frozen
    policy.} Under a uniform tail-gap assumption, we prove
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}) that the meta-algorithm's average reward
    strictly exceeds that of the best frozen expert, with a lower bound
    quantifying the multiplication factor explicitly.
    \item \textbf{Empirical validation across three regimes.} On a
    622{,}000-round entity-linking benchmark with three Flan-T5 predictors,
    we show that the meta-algorithm achieves strict reward multiplication
    precisely when the frozen policy is weaker than LinUCB's asymptotic
    ceiling (matching our tail-gap condition), fails gracefully when this
    condition is violated, and that the bulk of its advantage over cold
    LinUCB comes from the geometric warm-start effect predicted by the
    theory, not from direct early reward. We further demonstrate the same
    mechanism at scale on AmazonCat-13K (1.18M rounds, 13{,}330 actions),
    where the meta-algorithm breaks the frozen policy's accuracy ceiling
    and achieves a $\sim 4.8\times$ reward multiplication factor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

To formalize the phenomenon discussed above, we work in a standard contextual bandit setting \cite{zhu2022largeaction, zhu2022contextual}. At each round $t=1,\dots,T$, the learner observes a context and an action set $\mathcal{A}_t$, selects an action $a \in \mathcal{A}_t$, and receives bandit feedback. In the linear setting studied later, each action $a$ is associated with a feature vector $x_t(a)\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and the expected reward takes the form
\[
\mu_t(a)=x_t(a)^\top \theta^\star
\]
for an unknown parameter $\theta^\star\in\mathbb{R}^d$. The learner has access to two qualitatively different decision rules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \emph{fixed policy}, induced by some estimator or prior vector $\widehat{\theta}$, which does not change with online data;
    \item an \emph{adaptive bandit algorithm}, such as LinUCB, which updates its estimate over time and eventually improves through exploration.
\end{enumerate}
The fixed policy may be interpreted as coming from an LLM, pretraining, offline supervision, or another external source of prior knowledge. The key structural asymmetry is that the fixed policy may be good initially but cannot improve, whereas the adaptive algorithm may be weak initially but statistically improves as more data are collected.

In the next section, we instantiate this idea in a linear contextual bandit model with a fixed linear policy and an adaptive bandit algorithm to demonstrate how initialization impacts early exploration, and then study how to route decisions among multiple fixed policies.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:main-results}

This section develops the paper's main contributions. Section~\ref{sec:specific} gives a high-dimensional example isolating the geometric mechanism by which an initialization affects LinUCB's trajectory. Section~\ref{sec:master-multiple} introduces the confidence-gated meta-algorithm that routes decisions between frozen policies and online LinUCB. Section~\ref{sec:reward-multiplication} states the warm-started regret decomposition (Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}) and Section~\ref{sec:avg-reward-multiplication} translates it into an average-reward multiplication bound against the best frozen policy (Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}). Proofs of all results in this section are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{A Geometric Mechanism: Constant Policies and Warm-Started LinUCB}
\label{sec:specific}

We construct a linear bandit instance on which three natural strategies behave very differently: (i) a constant policy based on a fixed parameter estimate, (ii) LinUCB with isotropic initialization, and (iii) LinUCB warm-started by the data the constant policy would generate. The first two suffer linear regret, while their composition achieves constant regret with high probability. The point of the example is not that warm-starting helps, but that two individually inadequate components --- a non-learning prior policy and an explore-aggressive online algorithm --- combine to produce a strategy that neither attains alone. The phenomenon relies on a large ambient dimension and carefully structured context vectors.

\begin{proposition}[Three-way separation in high-dimensional linear bandits]
\label{prop:three-way}
Fix a horizon $n\ge 1$, a constant $L>1$, and a regularization parameter $\lambda>0$. There exist a dimension $d\ge n+1$, an unknown parameter $\theta^\star\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and a sequence of two-action sets $\{\mathcal{A}_t\}_{t=1}^n$ such that the following hold for any sub-Gaussian noise model with parameter $\sigma$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item there is a constant (non-updating) policy $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ whose cumulative pseudo-regret on the horizon $n$ is exactly $n$;
    \item LinUCB initialized with $V_0=\lambda I_d$ and $\widehat{\theta}_0=0$ also incurs cumulative pseudo-regret $n$, selecting the same action as $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ at every round;
    \item for any $m$ sufficiently large (in particular satisfying the explicit condition stated in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-three-way}), LinUCB warm-started with the design matrix that $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ would generate from $mn$ offline rollouts incurs cumulative pseudo-regret $O(1)$ with probability at least $1-\delta$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The construction, the supporting subgaussian concentration lemma, and the full proof are given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-three-way}.

The roles of the two components are complementary. The constant policy contributes no online exploitation but reshapes the geometry of $V_0$ in the directions LinUCB would otherwise over-explore; isotropic LinUCB contributes online exploitation but is misled into expending its budget on directions irrelevant to reward. Composed --- the constant policy as data collector, LinUCB as online learner --- they recover the reward-relevant direction after a single pull. This is the central qualitative point of the example: when the dimension is large relative to the horizon, the value of warm-start data is not that it estimates $\theta^\star$ (it doesn't --- the warm-started parameter satisfies $\widehat\theta_0'=0$), but that it constrains LinUCB's optimism enough for one good observation to settle the comparison.

\begin{remark}
This phenomenon is analogous to the classical multi-armed bandit setting, where an unpulled arm remains highly optimistic. In the contextual setting, such examples are less immediate because all actions share the same unknown parameter $\theta^\star$, so information can in principle transfer across arms. Here, the orthogonality of the bad directions prevents that transfer from helping. Note that the constant policy and isotropic LinUCB select the \emph{same} action at every round, but for very different reasons: $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ is hard-coded to favor the bad direction, whereas LinUCB is misled by isotropic uncertainty.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Mastering Multiple Constant Policies via a Confidence-Gated Meta-Algorithm}
\label{sec:master-multiple}

In the previous section, we showed that a better initialization of $V_0$
can substantially improve performance, especially in the short-horizon regime.
When the horizon $T$ is not large relative to the ambient dimension $d$,
a favorable initialization shrinks the initial confidence ellipsoid in
reward-relevant directions and can therefore accelerate reward accumulation.

In this section, we take an algorithmic perspective.
Rather than encoding prior knowledge through a single improved initialization,
we assume access to \emph{multiple frozen policy estimators}
$\{(\widehat{\theta}_i,V^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m$.
Each pair should be thought of as a constant policy obtained offline:
$\widehat{\theta}_i$ is a fixed linear predictor, and $V^{(i)}$ is the
corresponding design matrix that captures the geometry of the data on which
that predictor was trained.

Our goal is not to compete with the single best expert in hindsight.
That objective is closer in spirit to online model-selection methods such as
CORRAL \cite{agarwal2017corralling}, which are also used by
\cite{chen2024llmdecision} to randomly mix LLM-based policies with
contextual bandit algorithms.
Such methods are well suited for robustness guarantees of the form
``perform nearly as well as the best base algorithm.''
Here, however, our objective is different:
we want to exploit \emph{reward multiplication} from good frozen policies in the
early rounds, while still allowing the online learner to take over once it has
become sufficiently confident.
For that reason, we use a deterministic confidence-gated rule rather than a
randomized master algorithm.

\paragraph{Setting.}
At each round $t=1,\dots,T$, the learner observes a finite action set
$\mathcal{A}_t$.
Each action $a\in\mathcal{A}_t$ is associated with a feature vector
$x_{t,a}\in\mathbb{R}^d$.
The learner selects an action $A_t\in\mathcal{A}_t$ and observes reward
$r_t = \langle \theta^\star, x_{t,A_t}\rangle + \eta_t$,
where $\eta_t$ is conditionally mean-zero sub-Gaussian noise.
We are given $m$ frozen estimators $\{(\widehat{\theta}_i,V^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m$
and an online LinUCB learner with state $(V_t,b_t,\widehat{\theta}_t)$,
where $\widehat{\theta}_t = V_t^{-1} b_t$.
The frozen estimators are never updated; only LinUCB is updated online.

\paragraph{Expert proposals and geometry-aware confidence.}
Each frozen expert $i\in[m]$ proposes the greedy action
$a_t^{(i)} \in \arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_t} \langle \widehat{\theta}_i, x_{t,a}\rangle$.
To measure how trustworthy expert $i$ is on the current round, we evaluate the
uncertainty of its proposed action under its own design geometry:
\[
u_t^{(i)}
:=
\bigl\|x_{t,a_t^{(i)}}\bigr\|_{(V^{(i)})^{-1}},
\qquad
\|x\|_{M^{-1}} := \sqrt{x^\top M^{-1}x}.
\]
The interpretation is simple. If the proposed action lies in directions that
are well covered by the offline data underlying expert $i$, then $u_t^{(i)}$
is small and the expert is more trustworthy; if instead the current context
points in directions poorly represented in $V^{(i)}$, then $u_t^{(i)}$ is
large and the expert should be trusted less.
We treat LinUCB itself as an additional candidate, indexed by $0$, with proposal
\[
a_t^{(0)}
\in
\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_t}
\left\{
\langle \widehat{\theta}_t, x_{t,a}\rangle
+
\alpha_t \|x_{t,a}\|_{V_t^{-1}}
\right\},
\]
and uncertainty score $u_t^{(0)} := \|x_{t,a_t^{(0)}}\|_{V_t^{-1}}$.

\paragraph{Deterministic confidence-gated selection.}
A natural rule is to prefer the frozen expert whose proposed action has the
smallest uncertainty score, unless the online learner is already at least as
confident. Concretely, define $i_t^\star \in \arg\min_{i\in[m]} u_t^{(i)}$,
and choose
\[
I_t
=
\begin{cases}
i_t^\star, & \text{if } u_t^{(i_t^\star)} < u_t^{(0)},\\[3pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
A_t := a_t^{(I_t)}.
\]
Thus the master algorithm is entirely deterministic:
on each round it compares the most geometry-confident frozen policy against the
current LinUCB proposal and selects whichever source appears more reliable.
Early in learning, LinUCB typically has a large confidence radius, so one of
the frozen experts may be preferred. As data accumulate, the online design
matrix $V_t$ grows and the LinUCB uncertainty shrinks, and eventually the
adaptive learner becomes the most confident option and takes over automatically.

\paragraph{Optional permanent handover to LinUCB.}
To formalize the idea that LinUCB should eventually dominate, it is useful to
include a one-way switching criterion. For each frozen expert $i\in[m]$,
define an upper confidence score
\[
U_t^{(i)}
=
\langle \widehat{\theta}_i, x_{t,a_t^{(i)}}\rangle
+
\sqrt{\beta_i}\,
\|x_{t,a_t^{(i)}}\|_{(V^{(i)})^{-1}},
\]
and a lower confidence score for LinUCB
\[
L_t^{(0)}
=
\langle \widehat{\theta}_t, x_{t,a_t^{(0)}}\rangle
-
\sqrt{\beta_t}\,
\|x_{t,a_t^{(0)}}\|_{V_t^{-1}}.
\]
If for $K$ consecutive rounds we have $L_t^{(0)} \ge \max_{i\in[m]} U_t^{(i)}$,
then we permanently hand control over to LinUCB. This test expresses the
high-level principle that once the adaptive learner's lower confidence bound
exceeds every frozen expert's upper confidence bound, there is no longer any
reason to consult the frozen policies.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Confidence-Gated Master over Frozen Policies and LinUCB}
\label{alg:confidence-gated}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Frozen estimators $\{(\widehat{\theta}_i,V^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m$, regularization $\lambda>0$, confidence parameters $\{\alpha_t\}_{t=1}^T$
\State Initialize LinUCB state:
\[
V_1=\lambda I_d,\qquad b_1=0,\qquad \widehat{\theta}_1=0
\]
\State Set $\textsc{handover}\gets \mathrm{False}$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
    \State Observe action set $\mathcal{A}_t$
    \State Compute LinUCB proposal
    \[
    a_t^{(0)} \in \arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_t}
    \left\{
    \langle \widehat{\theta}_t,x_{t,a}\rangle
    + \alpha_t \|x_{t,a}\|_{V_t^{-1}}
    \right\}
    \]
    \State Set $u_t^{(0)} \gets \|x_{t,a_t^{(0)}}\|_{V_t^{-1}}$
    \For{$i=1,\dots,m$}
        \State Compute frozen proposal
        \[
        a_t^{(i)} \in \arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_t}
        \langle \widehat{\theta}_i,x_{t,a}\rangle
        \]
        \State Set
        \[
        u_t^{(i)} \gets \|x_{t,a_t^{(i)}}\|_{(V^{(i)})^{-1}}
        \]
    \EndFor
    \If{$\textsc{handover}=\mathrm{True}$}
        \State $A_t \gets a_t^{(0)}$
    \Else
        \State Choose
        \[
        i_t^\star \in \arg\min_{i\in[m]} u_t^{(i)}
        \]
        \If{$u_t^{(i_t^\star)} < u_t^{(0)}$}
            \State $A_t \gets a_t^{(i_t^\star)}$
        \Else
            \State $A_t \gets a_t^{(0)}$
        \EndIf
    \EndIf
    \State Observe reward $r_t$
    \State Update LinUCB:
    \[
    V_{t+1}\gets V_t + x_{t,A_t}x_{t,A_t}^\top,
    \qquad
    b_{t+1}\gets b_t + r_t x_{t,A_t},
    \qquad
    \widehat{\theta}_{t+1}\gets V_{t+1}^{-1}b_{t+1}
    \]
    \State Compute $U_t^{(i)}$ for all $i\in[m]$ and $L_t^{(0)}$
    \If{$L_t^{(0)} \ge \max_{i\in[m]} U_t^{(i)}$ for $K$ consecutive rounds}
        \State $\textsc{handover}\gets \mathrm{True}$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The key quantity in the master rule is not the frozen expert's predicted reward
alone, but its confidence-adjusted geometric fit to the current action set.
A frozen policy may be strong precisely because its training data produced a
design matrix that is well aligned with reward-relevant directions.
When this happens, the corresponding uncertainty score is small, and the master
can use that policy as a reliable warm start. Conversely, if the current
contexts fall outside the geometry captured by a frozen expert, its uncertainty
score will be large and the rule will defer to LinUCB. This perspective also
clarifies the distinction from expert-mixing approaches such as CORRAL: those
methods randomize among base algorithms in order to achieve robust regret
relative to the best base algorithm, whereas our goal is to use frozen policies
to improve the \emph{trajectory of learning} itself by steering early data
collection toward useful directions, thereby increasing short-horizon average
reward and amplifying the eventual performance of the adaptive learner.

\subsection{A Regret Decomposition for the Warm-Started Meta-Algorithm}
\label{sec:reward-multiplication}

The empirical phenomenon in \cite{chen2024llmdecision} suggests a useful
decomposition of the benefit of combining a frozen LLM-style policy with an
adaptive contextual bandit: (i) the frozen policy contributes \emph{good early
actions}, which immediately improve reward; and (ii) those same actions
contribute \emph{useful feature coverage}, so that when control is handed
over to LinUCB, the adaptive learner starts from a better-conditioned design
matrix and therefore learns faster.
The second effect is the source of the apparent ``multiplication'': the frozen
policy does not merely add reward on the rounds when it is played, it also
shrinks the future uncertainty term of the adaptive algorithm.

We now formalize this under a stylized warm-start model. Fix one frozen
expert $i^\star\in[m]$, and suppose the meta-algorithm follows
expert $i^\star$ for the first $\tau$ rounds and then permanently switches to
LinUCB. Write $a_t^\star \in \arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A_t}\langle \theta^\star, x_{t,a}\rangle$
for the optimal action, and define $\mu_t(a) := \langle \theta^\star, x_{t,a}\rangle$.
The instantaneous gap of expert $i^\star$ is
$\Delta_t^{(i^\star)} := \mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(a_t^{(i^\star)})$.
We impose the following assumptions.

\paragraph{Assumption A (uniform early quality).}
There exists $\varepsilon_0\ge 0$ such that for all $t\le \tau$,
$\Delta_t^{(i^\star)} \le \varepsilon_0$.
Thus expert $i^\star$ is uniformly near-optimal during the warm-start phase.

\paragraph{Assumption B (reward-relevant warm-start coverage).}
There exists an $r$-dimensional orthogonal projector $P$ such that
$\theta^\star \in \mathrm{Im}(P)$, $x_{t,a}\in \mathrm{Im}(P)$ for all $t,a$, and
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}
x_{t,a_t^{(i^\star)}}x_{t,a_t^{(i^\star)}}^\top
\succeq \nu \tau\, P
\]
for some $\nu>0$.
In words, the frozen expert collects data that covers the reward-relevant
subspace at rate $\nu\tau$.

\paragraph{Assumption C (correct handover).}
The confidence-gated switching rule triggers by time $\tau$, after which the
algorithm follows LinUCB exclusively.

Assumption B is the key structural condition. It says that the frozen expert is
not merely good in reward, but that it also expands the design matrix in the
subspace that matters for predicting reward. This is precisely the mechanism by
which a fixed policy can improve the future adaptive learner.

\begin{theorem}[Warm-started regret bound]
\label{thm:warm-start-regret}
Assume rewards follow the linear model
\[
r_t = \langle \theta^\star, x_{t,A_t}\rangle + \eta_t,
\]
where $(\eta_t)_t$ is conditionally $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, and
$\|x_{t,a}\|_2\le L$ for all $t,a$.
Suppose Assumptions A--C hold. Then with probability at least $1-\delta$,
the cumulative regret of the confidence-gated meta-algorithm satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warm-meta-regret}
R_T^{\mathrm{meta}}
\;\le\;
\tau\varepsilon_0
+
C\,\beta_T\,
\sqrt{
r (T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
},
\end{equation}
where $C>0$ is a universal constant and $\beta_T$ is the usual linear-bandit
confidence radius.
By comparison, a cold-start LinUCB bound takes the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cold-regret}
R_T^{\mathrm{cold}}
\;\le\;
C\,\beta_T\,
\sqrt{
rT
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2T}{\lambda}
\Bigr)
}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The proof, which separately bounds the warm-start phase using
Assumption~A and the post-handover LinUCB phase using a standard
self-normalized concentration argument applied to the enlarged design
matrix from Assumption~B, is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-warm-start}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret} cleanly separates the two benefits of the
frozen policy:
\[
\underbrace{\tau\varepsilon_0}_{\text{early additive term}}
\qquad+\qquad
\underbrace{
C\,\beta_T\,
\sqrt{
r (T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
}
}_{\text{late adaptive term}}.
\]
The early term measures how good the frozen policy is as a policy.
The late term measures how much it improves the \emph{initial condition} of
LinUCB through the enlarged matrix $\lambda+\nu\tau$ in the reward-relevant
subspace.
In particular, the adaptive part of the regret is reduced relative to cold-start
LinUCB by the factor
\[
\Psi(\tau,\nu)
:=
\sqrt{\frac{T-\tau}{T}}
\cdot
\sqrt{
\frac{
\log\!\bigl(1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}\bigr)
}{
\log\!\bigl(1+\frac{L^2T}{\lambda}\bigr)
}
}
\;\approx\;
\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\nu\tau}},
\]
ignoring horizon and logarithmic effects. Thus the warm-start phase
\emph{multiplies down} the future uncertainty cost of LinUCB.

\begin{corollary}[Strict improvement over the two baselines]
\label{cor:strict-improvement-clean}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}, suppose in
addition that the frozen expert has tail gap at least
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}>0$:
\[
\mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(a_t^{(i^\star)}) \ge \varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}
\qquad
\text{for all } t>\tau,
\]
and that
\[
\tau\varepsilon_0
<
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r\tau\log\!\Bigl(1+\frac{L^2\tau}{\lambda}\Bigr)
}
\]
and
\[
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r (T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}\Bigr)
}
<
(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}.
\]
Then
$R_T^{\mathrm{meta}} < \min\{R_T^{\mathrm{cold}},\,R_T^{(i^\star)}\}$.
Equivalently, the meta-algorithm obtains strictly larger cumulative reward
than either base algorithm run in isolation.
\end{corollary}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-strict-improvement}. The gain
from combining a frozen policy with LinUCB should be read as
``early additive gain $+$ late multiplicative shrinkage of uncertainty.''
This gives a concrete theoretical interpretation of the empirical phenomenon:
a good frozen policy not only earns reward early, it also preconditions the
design matrix so that the adaptive learner improves faster thereafter.

\subsection{Average-Reward Multiplication Against the Best Frozen Policy}
\label{sec:avg-reward-multiplication}

The empirical effect in \cite{chen2024llmdecision} is often more naturally
described in terms of average reward than regret. Define
$\bar r_T^{\alg} := \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb E[r_t^{\alg}]$.
Since $R_T^{\alg} = \sum_{t=1}^T \mu_t(a_t^\star) - T\bar r_T^{\alg}$,
a regret reduction is equivalent to an average-reward increase. However, to
capture the observed ``reward multiplication'' effect, it is more transparent
to compare the meta-algorithm directly to the best frozen policy.

No algorithm can guarantee a factor strictly larger than $1$ against
\emph{every} fixed policy without further assumptions: if some frozen
policy is already optimal on every round, then no other algorithm can
uniformly guarantee a larger average reward. Therefore, any nontrivial
multiplication result must assume that the frozen policies are useful early
but eventually suboptimal. To formalize this, for each frozen expert
$i\in[m]$ let
$a_t^{(i)} \in \arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A_t} \langle \widehat\theta_i, x_{t,a}\rangle$ and
$\bar r_T^{(i)} := \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb E[\mu_t(a_t^{(i)})]$.
Let $i^\star \in \arg\max_{i\in[m]} \bar r_T^{(i)}$ and
$\bar r_T^{\fix}:=\bar r_T^{(i^\star)}$. We retain Assumptions A--C from
Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}, now applied to the best frozen expert
$i^\star$, and add the following.

\paragraph{Assumption D (uniform tail gap for frozen experts).}
There exists $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}>0$ such that for every frozen
expert $i\in[m]$,
\[
\frac{1}{T-\tau}\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T
\bigl(
\mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(a_t^{(i)})
\bigr)
\ge
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}.
\]
In words, every frozen policy is eventually worse than the optimal adaptive
behavior by at least $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}$ on average over the tail.
This is the assumption that turns a regret improvement into a genuine
``multiplication over fixed policies'' statement.

\begin{theorem}[Average-reward multiplication against the best frozen policy]
\label{thm:avg-mult}
Under Assumptions A--D, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:avg-reward-improvement}
\bar r_T^{\meta}
\;\ge\;
\bar r_T^{\fix}
+
\frac{(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}}{T}
-
\frac{C\,\beta_T}{T}
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, whenever $\bar r_T^{\fix}>0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multiplication-lower-bound}
\frac{\bar r_T^{\meta}}{\bar r_T^{\fix}}
\;\ge\;
1+
\frac{
(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}
-
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\bigl(1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}\bigr)
}
}{
T\,\bar r_T^{\fix}
}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The proof, which combines the post-handover regret bound from
Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret} with the tail-gap inequality of
Assumption~D, is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-avg-mult}.

A simple sufficient condition for strict multiplication is immediate:
whenever
\[
(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}
>
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
},
\]
we have $\bar r_T^{\meta} > \bar r_T^{\fix}$, so the meta-algorithm achieves
strictly larger average reward than the best frozen policy in hindsight.
Because the post-handover LinUCB term is sublinear in $T-\tau$ while the
frozen-policy tail gap contributes a linear gain
$(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}$, the improvement becomes especially
transparent for large horizons: whenever $\tau=o(T)$ and $\bar r_T^{\fix}$
stays bounded away from zero,
\[
\frac{\bar r_T^{\meta}}{\bar r_T^{\fix}}
\ge
1+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}}{\bar r_T^{\fix}}-o(1).
\]
The multiplication factor is large when (i) the frozen policy is strong
enough to be useful in the warm-start phase; (ii) the frozen policies are
eventually dominated by online learning, so that
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}>0$; and (iii) the actions selected in the
warm-start phase generate good coverage in the reward-relevant subspace, so
that $\nu\tau$ is large and the later LinUCB regret is small. In this sense,
the combined algorithm beats a fixed policy twice: first through better
early decisions, and second by using those decisions to build a better
design matrix for future learning.

\textbf{Datasets.} We evaluate the empirical performance of our Value-Gated architecture on two large-scale textual contextual bandit datasets characterized by massive action spaces, summarized in Table \ref{tab:datasets}. We formulate the objective as a strict Precision@1 task: the algorithm receives a binary reward of $r_t = 1$ for selecting a correct ground-truth action, and $r_t = 0$ otherwise.

\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{Time Horizon ($T$)} & \textbf{Action Space ($|A|$)} \\ \midrule

\textbf{Baselines \& Meta-Algorithm.} Each experiment simultaneously tracks three policies on the same data stream:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Router (Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}):} The confidence-gated meta-algorithm. We model the frozen LLM's design matrix as $V^{(1)} = c \cdot I_d$ with confidence parameter $c$, simplifying the LLM uncertainty score to $u_t^{(1)} = \|\phi(x_t, a_t^{(1)})\|_2 / \sqrt{c}$. The adaptive LinUCB's uncertainty is $u_t^{(0)} = \|\phi(x_t, a_t^{(0)})\|_{V_t^{-1}}$. The deterministic routing rule selects the LLM when $u_t^{(1)} < u_t^{(0)}$ and LinUCB otherwise.
    \item \textbf{LLM only (Pure Master):} A static frozen policy applied on every round, establishing the natural accuracy boundary.
    \item \textbf{Cold LinUCB (Pure Apprentice):} An independent LinUCB instance initialized from $\lambda I_d$ with no warm-start data.
\end{enumerate}

Regardless of which policy is selected by the Router, the adaptive learner updates its design matrix $V$ and response vector $b$ with the played action and observed reward. 

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.} We measure learning efficiency using the Cumulative Average Reward (Precision@1). Mechanistically, we track the Fraction of LLM Routing over time to explicitly visualize the algorithm's ability to decay its reliance on the computationally expensive LLM.

\subsection{Performance on OneShotWikiLinks-311}
\label{sec:wiki_experiments}

\paragraph{Environment Configuration.}
The OneShotWikiLinks-311 benchmark consists of 622{,}000 context--entity pairs spanning 311 entities (filtered for $\geq$ 2{,}000 support). Context embeddings are produced by a frozen SentenceTransformer (\texttt{bert-base-nli-mean-tokens}), yielding $768$-dimensional vectors. Each entity is similarly embedded, producing a fixed action feature matrix $\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{311 \times 768}$. The joint feature is the element-wise product $\phi(x,a) = x \odot a \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$. The frozen estimator is a Flan-T5 language model \cite{chung2024scaling} used in mask-filling mode: the masked context is passed through T5, the predicted token is embedded, and the entity with highest cosine similarity to the prediction is selected. We experiment with three model sizes---\texttt{small} (60M parameters), \texttt{base} (250M), and \texttt{large} (780M)---which achieve progressively higher accuracy but are never updated online. The adaptive algorithm is LinUCB with Sherman--Morrison rank-1 updates, ridge regularization $\lambda = 0.1$, and geometric decay $\alpha_t = \max(0.1,\, 1.0 \cdot 0.9985^t)$. Data arrive in batches of 128 samples (4{,}860 batches total). To ensure a controlled comparison across LLM regimes, all methods share the same fixed random seed and data ordering, so that the Cold LinUCB baseline produces identical reward trajectories regardless of which LLM is paired with the Router. The confidence parameter is set uniformly to $c=100$ across all three LLM sizes; this eliminates $c$ as a confound and ensures that any performance differences across regimes are attributable solely to the frozen policy's accuracy, not to per-regime hyperparameter tuning.

\paragraph{Main Result: Reward Multiplication.}
To validate Corollary~\ref{cor:strict-improvement-clean}, we deploy the Router using a \texttt{small} (60M parameter) Flan-T5 model ($c=100$) as the frozen policy. Flan-T5-small achieves 7.59\% accuracy, well below LinUCB's asymptotic ceiling of approximately 10.7\%. The performance dynamics are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:wiki-small-learning}. The Router defers to the LLM for the initial phase of learning: LLM routing begins at near 100\%, drops below 50\% by batch~30, falls below 10\% by batch~150, and ultimately settles to 0.3\% of total rounds. During this early window, passive updates precondition LinUCB's design matrix. By batch~140 the Router's cumulative reward surpasses the LLM ceiling, and by batch~230 even Cold LinUCB catches up---but the warm-started Router maintains a persistent lead throughout the remaining horizon. After the hand-off, the warm-started LinUCB converges to a final accuracy of 10.83\%, strictly exceeding both the LLM baseline (7.59\%) and Cold LinUCB (10.69\%). This confirms the primary theoretical claim: an adaptive learner guided dynamically by a weak Master achieves strict reward multiplication.

\subsection{Large-Scale Exhibition on AmazonCat-13K}
\label{sec:amazon_experiments}

To test the limits of our theoretical framework, we scale the environment to the AmazonCat-13K dataset, expanding the action space to $K = 13,330$ dimensions. In standard contextual bandits, mapping complex text contexts jointly with massive action spaces often causes catastrophic initial regret. To effectively navigate this space and prevent ``dimensional warp,'' we strictly separate textual context processing from geometric learning.

\paragraph{Unwarping the Feature Space.}
To make exploration computationally and statistically tractable for the adaptive learner, the high-dimensional label space is projected into a dense, continuous latent space $\mathbb{R}^d$ (where $d=50$) using truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). This yields a global feature representation $z_a \in \mathbb{R}^{50}$ for each action $a$. During the online phase, the adaptive learner (LinUCB) operates entirely within this reduced coordinate space, relying purely on the global label geometry $z_a$ rather than a dynamically warped, context-dependent feature vector.

\paragraph{Instantiating the Meta-Algorithm with Internal Confidence.}
We deploy the Confidence-Gated Meta-Algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}) using a single frozen expert ($m=1$), the Flan-T5 model. For this extreme multi-label setting, computing and maintaining an explicit offline design matrix $V^{(1)}$ for the LLM is computationally prohibitive. Instead, we introduce an efficient adaptation utilizing the LLM's internal generation probabilities.

Let $c_{\mathrm{LLM}} \in [0, 1]$ be the Master's internal confidence score for its predicted action $a_t^{(1)}$. We define the frozen policy's uncertainty score as a calibrated proxy:
\begin{equation}
    u_t^{(1)} = \lambda_{\mathrm{scale}} \cdot (1 - c_{\mathrm{LLM}})
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{scale}} \in (0, 1]$ is a micro-scale hyperparameter (e.g., $0.050$). This proxy efficiently replaces the geometric norm computation (Line 11 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}). It directly maps the Master's predictive ``doubt'' into a compatible scalar, allowing it to be evaluated against LinUCB's geometric variance $u_t^{(0)} = \|z_{a_t^{(0)}}\|_{V_t^{-1}}$ at the decision gate (Line 18).

If the Master wins the gate ($u_t^{(1)} < u_t^{(0)}$), its contextually accurate prediction is played. Regardless of which agent drives, LinUCB acts as a passive observer, updating its design matrix $V_t$ using the feature vector of the played action ($z_{A_t}$). Because the Master makes highly accurate contextual choices early on, it provides a guided geometric warm-start, causing the Apprentice's confidence ellipsoid to shrink rapidly along the true contours of the data until it is mathematically ready to take control.

The pure LLM baseline provides a static ceiling (0.059), while the Cold LinUCB bandit struggles to learn the massive label space from scratch. Our Confidence-Gated Master-Apprentice (Alg 1) leverages the LLM for a geometric warm-start, seamlessly handing over control to break the teacher's ceiling and achieve a final reward of $\sim 0.285$.}

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Empirical Results: Breaking the Teacher's Ceiling.}
The performance dynamics in this extreme multi-label environment are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:amazon_learning_curve}. The pure policy extremes effectively collapse under the scale of the task. The pure LinUCB Apprentice is blinded by the massive dimensionality, struggling to reach even a 0.04 reward after 1.18 million steps. The pure LLM (Flan-T5-small) establishes a strong initial prior but hits a hard accuracy boundary, flatlining at a static ceiling of 0.059.

Conversely, our meta-algorithm perfectly executes the theoretical prediction of Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}. By trusting the Master early, the algorithm avoids the catastrophic initial regret of the cold-start Bandit. Once the Apprentice's mathematical map is sufficiently preconditioned via the Master's early actions, $u_t^{(0)}$ decays below $u_t^{(1)}$, and the Apprentice smoothly takes the wheel---a transition visible in Figure~\ref{fig:amazon_learning_curve} as the orange curve crosses the LLM ceiling at approximately $t = 25{,}000$ steps and diverges sharply thereafter. Because its design matrix was geometrically warm-started, it learns substantially faster than the cold baseline, breaking entirely through the Master's natural accuracy ceiling. It achieves a final cumulative Precision@1 of $\sim 0.285$, yielding $\bar{r}^{\mathrm{meta}}/\bar{r}^{\mathrm{fix}} \approx 0.285/0.059 \approx 4.83$---a ${\sim}4.8\times$ reward multiplication factor consistent with the lower bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}, and far exceeding what either component achieves in isolation.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Discussion}
\label{sec:cross_dataset}

Taken together, the OneShotWikiLinks-311 and AmazonCat-13K experiments validate the theoretical predictions of Theorems~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret} and~\ref{thm:avg-mult} across two qualitatively different operating regimes.

\paragraph{Same mechanism, different magnitudes.}
In both settings the core mechanism is identical: the frozen policy's early actions precondition the LinUCB design matrix in reward-relevant directions (Assumption~B), the confidence-gated selector hands control to the adaptive learner once its geometric uncertainty shrinks below the frozen policy's (Assumption~C), and the warm-started LinUCB converges faster than a cold-start baseline. The magnitude of the effect, however, is governed by the tail gap $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}$ (Assumption~D). On OneShotWikiLinks-311, where the LLM and LinUCB ceilings are close, the multiplication factor is modest (${\sim}1.01\times$ for \texttt{small}). On AmazonCat-13K, where LinUCB's ceiling (${\sim}0.285$) vastly exceeds the LLM's ($0.059$), the tail gap is large and the multiplication factor reaches ${\sim}4.8\times$. This contrast directly mirrors Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}: the reward-multiplication lower bound grows with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}} / \bar{r}_T^{\mathrm{fix}}$, and the two datasets sit at opposite ends of this spectrum.

\paragraph{Robustness of the routing rule.}
The confidence-gated selector operates without any dataset-specific tuning of the routing logic. On Wiki, the LLM uncertainty is modeled geometrically via $V^{(1)} = cI$; on Amazon, it is replaced by a calibrated confidence proxy $\lambda_{\mathrm{scale}}(1 - c_{\mathrm{LLM}})$. Despite these different instantiations of Line~11 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}, the qualitative behavior is the same: the LLM dominates early, routing decays monotonically, and LinUCB takes over permanently. This suggests that the deterministic confidence-gated rule is robust to the specific form of the frozen-policy uncertainty estimate, so long as the estimate is calibrated to be comparable in scale to LinUCB's self-normalized uncertainty.

\paragraph{When does multiplication fail?}
The Wiki ablation provides a controlled answer: when the frozen policy exceeds the adaptive learner's ceiling (\texttt{large} regime), Assumption~D fails and the Router cannot surpass the LLM. Nevertheless, even in this regime the Router outperforms Cold LinUCB, confirming that the warm-start effect (the multiplicative reduction $\Psi(\tau,\nu)$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}) persists regardless of whether full reward multiplication is achieved. The AmazonCat-13K experiment does not exhibit this failure mode because the frozen policy is weak relative to LinUCB's capacity in the SVD-reduced space, placing it firmly in the Assumption~D-holds regime.

\paragraph{Distinction from concurrent approaches.}
Our experiments also clarify the design-space differences between the confidence-gated meta-algorithm and two concurrent lines of work that combine LLMs with contextual bandits.

\cite{chen2024llmdecision} arbitrate between the LLM and the bandit via a randomized CORRAL master \cite{agarwal2017corralling}: each base algorithm maintains a probability weight that grows with its accumulated reward, and on every round the master samples from this distribution. Because the mixing is stochastic and reward-proportional, it guarantees near-optimal performance relative to the single best base algorithm in hindsight but does not exploit per-sample geometric information. By contrast, our Router evaluates the self-normalized confidence radius of each policy on \emph{every individual context} and deterministically selects the source with the smallest uncertainty. This sample-level routing explicitly forces the LLM's early actions into reward-relevant directions of the LinUCB design matrix, producing the geometric warm-start that drives the multiplicative regret reduction of Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}---an effect that a probability-shifting framework is not designed to isolate. The three-regime ablation on OneShotWikiLinks-311 (Table~\ref{tab:results}) provides direct evidence: even in the \texttt{large} regime where Assumption~D fails, the deterministic Router still outperforms Cold LinUCB by $+0.28$ percentage points thanks to the warm-start, whereas a randomized master that increasingly favors the LLM would not transfer any geometric benefit to the bandit's design matrix.

\cite{huangcontextual} take a complementary ``early-fusion'' approach: they structurally merge the LLM's prior into the bandit's reward model by appending a trainable adapter layer to the LLM and updating it online to calibrate the prior as the environment shifts. While this tight coupling can exploit richer reward signals, it requires gradient-based updates to the LLM backbone (or its adapter) on every round, incurring non-trivial computational overhead that scales with model size. Our meta-algorithm instead treats the LLM as a strictly frozen, zero-shot policy---no adapter, no gradient updates, no modification of any LLM layer. All online learning is confined to the separate LinUCB apprentice, whose Sherman--Morrison rank-1 updates cost $O(d^2)$ per round regardless of LLM size. This ``late-fusion'' separation is what enables the clean regret decomposition in Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}: the frozen policy contributes a bounded additive early-reward term ($\tau\varepsilon_0$), while the warm-started adaptive term inherits the multiplicative uncertainty reduction $\Psi(\tau,\nu)\approx\sqrt{\lambda/(\lambda+\nu\tau)}$. The AmazonCat-13K experiment (Figure~\ref{fig:amazon_learning_curve}) demonstrates that this zero-cost-to-the-LLM design still achieves a ${\sim}4.8\times$ reward multiplication factor, confirming that active calibration of the LLM's internal layers is not necessary to obtain the geometric warm-start benefit.

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
These experiments suggest that the confidence-gated meta-algorithm is most valuable when (i) a pretrained model provides useful but imperfect prior knowledge, (ii) sufficient data exist for an adaptive learner to eventually surpass the frozen policy, and (iii) the action or feature space is large enough that cold-start exploration is prohibitively slow. The key empirical finding across both datasets is that the benefit is not merely from switching to the better policy at the right time, but from the geometric warm-start that preconditions the adaptive learner---a mechanism whose strength scales with frozen-policy quality (Figure~\ref{fig:wiki-lift}) and whose payoff grows with the tail gap (Figure~\ref{fig:amazon_learning_curve}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied the \emph{reward multiplication} phenomenon in which combining
a frozen pretrained policy with an adaptive contextual bandit learner
yields performance strictly exceeding either component in isolation.
Our contribution is a regret-theoretic explanation for this effect in
linear contextual bandits: a frozen policy should be understood not as a
competitor but as a mechanism that reshapes the geometry of the LinUCB
design matrix. Concretely, we gave (i) a high-dimensional example
(Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}) in which an anisotropic warm start
enables persistent selection of the optimal action while an isotropic
start persistently fails; (ii) a deterministic confidence-gated
meta-algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:confidence-gated}) that routes between
one or more frozen experts and LinUCB using self-normalized uncertainty
scores; (iii) a regret decomposition (Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret})
whose adaptive term is multiplicatively smaller than cold-start LinUCB by
the factor $\sqrt{\lambda/(\lambda+\nu\tau)}$ in reward-relevant directions;
and (iv) an average-reward multiplication bound
(Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}) against the best frozen policy, contingent
on a tail-gap condition that we verify empirically.
Experiments on OneShotWikiLinks with three Flan-T5 predictors confirm
that the multiplication factor materializes precisely when the tail-gap
condition holds, and that the bulk of the advantage over cold LinUCB
stems from the geometric warm-start effect rather than direct early
reward. A large-scale exhibition on AmazonCat-13K (1.18M rounds,
13{,}330 actions) further shows the meta-algorithm scaling to extreme
multi-label settings, breaking the frozen policy's accuracy ceiling by
roughly $4.8\times$.

\paragraph{Limitations and future directions.}
Our analysis assumes linear reward and coverage in a fixed
reward-relevant subspace. Extending these results to generalized linear
and neural-linear bandits, where the feature map itself is learned, is
a natural next step; the routing criterion generalizes immediately, but
the geometric warm-start argument does not. A second limitation is that
Assumption~D is unverifiable online---our empirical ``Regime 3'' shows
the meta-algorithm can underperform a very strong frozen policy when
this assumption fails, suggesting the value of an anytime test for
tail-gap violation and a safer routing rule that defers to the frozen
policy when such a test fires. Finally, our experiments use a single
frozen expert; leveraging multiple heterogeneous frozen policies---for
example, multiple prompts, multiple embedding models, or multiple LLM
sizes---and characterizing when the meta-algorithm benefits from the
ensemble geometry is a promising direction that the theory already
accommodates.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}}
\label{app:proof-three-way}

We first record the standard subgaussian concentration lemma used in the
high-probability part of the argument, and then construct the linear bandit
instance and verify the three claims of Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}.

\begin{lemma}[Subgaussian concentration, Corollary 5.5 of \cite{lattimore2020bandit}]
\label{lem:subgaussian}
Assume that $X_1-\mu,\dots,X_n-\mu$ are independent, $\sigma$-subgaussian random variables. Then for any $\varepsilon \ge 0$,
\[
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\hat{\mu} \ge \mu+\varepsilon\right)
\le
\exp\!\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\qquad
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\hat{\mu} \le \mu-\varepsilon\right)
\le
\exp\!\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\]
where
\[
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n X_t.
\]
\end{lemma}

\subparagraph{Problem setup.}
Let the dimension satisfy $d \ge n+1$, where $n$ is the horizon. The unknown parameter is
\[
\theta^\star = e_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d,
\]
where $\{e_i\}_{i=1}^d$ denotes the standard basis. Rewards follow the stochastic linear model
\[
X_t = \langle \theta^\star, A_t \rangle + \eta_t,
\]
where $\eta_t$ are independent mean-zero $\sigma$-subgaussian random variables.

At each round $t=1,\dots,n$, the learner is offered two actions,
\[
g:=e_1,
\qquad
b_t:=L e_{t+1},
\]
where $L>1$ is a fixed constant. Their expected rewards are
\[
\langle \theta^\star,g\rangle = 1,
\qquad
\langle \theta^\star,b_t\rangle = 0.
\]
Both LinUCB variants below use the rule
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_t(a)
=
a^\top \hat{\theta}_{t-1}
+
\beta \|a\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}},
\]
with
\[
V_{t-1}=V_0+\sum_{s<t} A_sA_s^\top,
\qquad
\hat{\theta}_{t-1}
=
V_{t-1}^{-1}\sum_{s<t} A_s X_s.
\]

\subparagraph{Algorithm 1: a constant policy.}
Consider a policy $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ that selects an action by maximizing $\langle \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}}, a\rangle$ for some fixed parameter estimate $\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ that is never updated. For concreteness, take
\[
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}}
=
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=2}^{n+1} e_j,
\]
so that
\[
\langle \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}},g\rangle = 0,
\qquad
\langle \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}},b_t\rangle = 1
\quad\text{for every } t\le n.
\]
Then $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ selects $b_t$ at every round $t$. Since each pull of $b_t$ yields expected reward $0$ while $g$ has expected reward $1$, the cumulative pseudo-regret of $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ on the horizon $n$ is exactly $n$. This proves part (1) of Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}.

The failure mode is structural rather than statistical: $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ never queries direction $e_1$, so no amount of data can refine $\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{const}}$ in the reward-relevant direction. The policy has no learning mechanism. We will see, however, that the trajectory it produces is precisely what suppresses the misplaced optimism of LinUCB in the bad directions.

\subparagraph{Algorithm 2: LinUCB with isotropic initialization.}
The first LinUCB variant uses
\[
V_0=\lambda I_d,
\qquad
\hat{\theta}_0=0.
\]
At $t=1$,
\[
\|g\|_{V_0^{-1}}^2=\frac{1}{\lambda},
\qquad
\|b_1\|_{V_0^{-1}}^2=\frac{L^2}{\lambda}.
\]
Since $\hat{\theta}_0=0$, we have
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_1(b_1)>\mathrm{UCB}_1(g),
\]
so the algorithm selects $b_1$.

Now suppose inductively that the algorithm has selected $b_1,\dots,b_{t-1}$ in the first $t-1$ rounds. Then
\[
V_{t-1}
=
\lambda I_d+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1} b_s b_s^\top
=
\lambda I_d + L^2\sum_{j=2}^{t} e_j e_j^\top.
\]
Hence $V_{t-1}$ is diagonal in the standard basis. In particular, for the fresh direction $e_{t+1}$,
\[
e_{t+1}^\top V_{t-1}^{-1} e_{t+1}=\frac{1}{\lambda},
\qquad
\|b_t\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^2=\frac{L^2}{\lambda},
\qquad
\|g\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^2=\frac{1}{\lambda}.
\]

The key point is that although the observed rewards on $b_1,\dots,b_{t-1}$ contain noise, the estimate
\[
\hat{\theta}_{t-1}
=
V_{t-1}^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1} b_s X_s
\]
lies entirely in $\mathrm{span}\{e_2,\dots,e_t\}$. Therefore,
\[
g^\top \hat{\theta}_{t-1}=0,
\qquad
b_t^\top \hat{\theta}_{t-1}=0,
\]
because both $g=e_1$ and the fresh arm $b_t=L e_{t+1}$ are orthogonal to $\mathrm{span}\{e_2,\dots,e_t\}$.

Thus, even in the stochastic-reward setting, the comparison between $g$ and $b_t$ is determined purely by the confidence terms:
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_t(g)=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\lambda}},
\qquad
\mathrm{UCB}_t(b_t)=\frac{\beta L}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.
\]
Since $L>1$, we have $\mathrm{UCB}_t(b_t)>\mathrm{UCB}_t(g)$ for all $t\le n$. Hence the isotropically initialized algorithm selects $b_t$ at every round and, like $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$, incurs cumulative pseudo-regret $n$. This proves part (2) of Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}.

\subparagraph{Algorithm 3: LinUCB with an anisotropic warm start.}
We now define an initialization that is realized by exactly the trajectory $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ would produce. Fix an integer $m\ge 1$, and let
\[
V_0'
=
\lambda I_d + mL^2 \sum_{j=2}^{n+1} e_j e_j^\top,
\qquad
\hat{\theta}_0'=0.
\]
This corresponds to having observed each bad direction $L e_j$, $j=2,\dots,n+1$, exactly $m$ times before the online interaction begins --- precisely the kind of data $\pi_{\mathrm{const}}$ generates if rolled out for $mn$ rounds on a sequence of action sets containing each bad direction. Thus $V_0'$ is a genuine design matrix obtainable from a collection of outer products produced by a constant logging policy.

At $t=1$,
\[
\|g\|_{(V_0')^{-1}}^2=\frac{1}{\lambda},
\qquad
\|b_1\|_{(V_0')^{-1}}^2
=
L^2\cdot \frac{1}{\lambda+mL^2}
=
\frac{L^2}{\lambda+mL^2}.
\]
Hence, if
\[
m > \frac{\lambda(L^2-1)}{L^2},
\]
then $\frac{1}{\lambda}>\frac{L^2}{\lambda+mL^2}$, and therefore
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_1'(g)>\mathrm{UCB}_1'(b_1).
\]
So the warm-started algorithm selects $g$ at round $1$.

Suppose now that the algorithm continues to select $g$ for the first $s$ rounds. Then
\[
V_s'
=
\lambda I_d
+
mL^2 \sum_{j=2}^{n+1} e_j e_j^\top
+
s\, e_1 e_1^\top.
\]
Since each reward observed on $g$ has mean $1$,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^s X_i = s + \sum_{i=1}^s \eta_i,
\]
and therefore
\[
g^\top \hat{\theta}_s'
=
e_1^\top (V_s')^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^s g X_i
=
\frac{1}{\lambda+s}\sum_{i=1}^s X_i.
\]
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:subgaussian} with $\mu=1$, we obtain that with probability at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for all $1\le s\le n$,
\[
\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^s X_i
\ge
1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\log(2n/\delta)}{s}}.
\]
On this event,
\[
g^\top \hat{\theta}_s'
\ge
\frac{s}{\lambda+s}
\left(
1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\log(2n/\delta)}{s}}
\right).
\]

For every fresh bad arm $b_{s+1}=Le_{s+2}$,
\[
b_{s+1}^\top \hat{\theta}_s' = 0,
\]
since the online trajectory has only sampled the direction $e_1$, and the warm-start contributes only to the design matrix, not to the response vector. Moreover,
\[
\|b_{s+1}\|_{(V_s')^{-1}}^2
=
\frac{L^2}{\lambda+mL^2},
\qquad
\|g\|_{(V_s')^{-1}}^2
=
\frac{1}{\lambda+s}.
\]

On the same high-probability event,
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_{s+1}'(g)
\ge
\frac{s}{\lambda+s}
\left(
1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\log(2n/\delta)}{s}}
\right)
+
\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\lambda+s}},
\]
while
\[
\mathrm{UCB}_{s+1}'(b_{s+1})
=
\frac{\beta L}{\sqrt{\lambda+mL^2}}.
\]
Consequently, if $m$ is chosen large enough so that for every $1\le s\le n$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:m-condition}
\frac{s}{\lambda+s}
\left(
1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\log(2n/\delta)}{s}}
\right)
+
\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\lambda+s}}
>
\frac{\beta L}{\sqrt{\lambda+mL^2}},
\end{equation}
then, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the warm-started algorithm selects $g$ at every round, and the cumulative pseudo-regret over the $n$ online rounds is $O(1)$. This proves part (3) of Proposition~\ref{prop:three-way}; condition~\eqref{eq:m-condition} is the explicit ``$m$ sufficiently large'' requirement referenced in the proposition statement.\hfill$\square$

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}}
\label{app:proof-warm-start}

\begin{proof}
Split the regret into the first $\tau$ rounds and the remaining rounds.

For the warm-start phase, Assumption A gives
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\bigl(\mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(A_t)\bigr)
\le
\tau\varepsilon_0.
\]

Now consider the post-handover phase. At time $\tau+1$, the LinUCB design
matrix is
\[
V_{\tau+1}
=
\lambda I + \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}
x_{t,a_t^{(i^\star)}}x_{t,a_t^{(i^\star)}}^\top.
\]
By Assumption B,
\[
V_{\tau+1}\succeq \lambda I + \nu\tau P.
\]
Since all action vectors lie in $\mathrm{Im}(P)$, the effective problem is
$r$-dimensional and each reward-relevant direction has effective regularization
at least $\lambda+\nu\tau$.

On the standard self-normalized concentration event, LinUCB satisfies for every
$t>\tau$,
\[
\mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(A_t)
\le
2\beta_T \|x_{t,A_t}\|_{V_t^{-1}}.
\]
Summing over $t=\tau+1,\dots,T$ and applying Cauchy--Schwarz yields
\[
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T
\bigl(\mu_t(a_t^\star)-\mu_t(A_t)\bigr)
\le
2\beta_T
\sqrt{
(T-\tau)\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \|x_{t,A_t}\|_{V_t^{-1}}^2
}.
\]
By the elliptical potential lemma,
\[
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \|x_{t,A_t}\|_{V_t^{-1}}^2
\le
2\log\frac{\det(V_{T+1})}{\det(V_{\tau+1})}.
\]
Using $\|x_{t,a}\|_2\le L$ and $V_{\tau+1}\succeq (\lambda+\nu\tau)P+\lambda(I-P)$,
we obtain
\[
\log\frac{\det(V_{T+1})}{\det(V_{\tau+1})}
\le
r\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr).
\]
Combining the last three displays proves \eqref{eq:warm-meta-regret}.
The cold-start bound \eqref{eq:cold-regret} is the same argument with
initial matrix $\lambda I$ and horizon $T$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:strict-improvement-clean}}
\label{app:proof-strict-improvement}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality says the frozen expert beats cold-start LinUCB on the
first $\tau$ rounds. The second says warm-started LinUCB beats the frozen
expert on the remaining $T-\tau$ rounds. The claim follows by comparing the
decomposition in Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret} to the corresponding
bounds for the two standalone baselines.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:avg-mult}}
\label{app:proof-avg-mult}

\begin{proof}
During the first $\tau$ rounds, the meta-algorithm and the chosen frozen
expert $i^\star$ coincide, so there is no difference in reward relative to
that expert on the warm-start phase.

Now consider the tail $t>\tau$. By Assumption D,
\[
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \mathbb E[\mu_t(a_t^{(i^\star)})]
\le
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \mu_t(a_t^\star)
-
(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}.
\]
On the other hand, by Theorem~\ref{thm:warm-start-regret}, the post-handover
LinUCB phase incurs regret at most
\[
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \mathbb E[r_t^{\meta}]
\ge
\sum_{t=\tau+1}^T \mu_t(a_t^\star)
-
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
}.
\]
Subtracting the frozen-policy bound from the meta bound yields
\[
T\bar r_T^{\meta}-T\bar r_T^{\fix}
\ge
(T-\tau)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}
-
C\,\beta_T
\sqrt{
r(T-\tau)
\log\!\Bigl(
1+\frac{L^2(T-\tau)}{\lambda+\nu\tau}
\Bigr)
},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:avg-reward-improvement}. Dividing by
$T\bar r_T^{\fix}$ gives \eqref{eq:multiplication-lower-bound}.
\end{proof}


\section{Extended Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This appendix expands on the related-work discussion deferred from the introduction. We cover (i) linear contextual bandits and confidence geometry, (ii) model selection and expert aggregation, (iii) warm starts and prior information, (iv) the geometry of information gain in linear bandits, and (v) large language models for sequential decision making.

Sequential decision making with contextual information has been studied
extensively in the contextual bandit literature, where the learner must
balance exploration and exploitation under partial feedback
\cite{langford2007epoch,agarwal2014taming,foster2021statistical,
lattimore2020bandit}.
In the linear setting, algorithms such as LinUCB and OFUL achieve regret
bounds that depend explicitly on the geometry of the accumulated design
matrix through self-normalized confidence ellipsoids
\cite{chu2011contextual,abbasi2011improved}.
Subsequent work has developed statistically efficient contextual bandit
algorithms under general function approximation assumptions
\cite{agarwal2012contextual,foster2018practical,foster2020beyond,
simchi2022bypassing,zhu2022contextual,rucker2023infinite,
zhang2024efficient}.
A central insight of this line of work is that the conditioning of the
covariance matrix determines how quickly uncertainty shrinks and therefore
how rapidly regret decreases. Our work builds on this perspective by showing
that actions selected by a frozen predictor can reshape this geometry early
in learning and accelerate subsequent adaptation.

\paragraph{Model selection and expert aggregation in bandits.}
Another closely related direction studies how to combine multiple candidate
policies or base algorithms online.
Classical expert-learning methods provide regret guarantees relative to the
best fixed expert in hindsight \cite{auer2002nonstochastic}.
In the bandit setting, CORRAL \cite{agarwal2017corralling} uses Online
Mirror Descent with a log-barrier regularizer and a carefully designed
learning-rate schedule to achieve regret competitive with the best base
algorithm; subsequent refinements tighten the rates and extend the
framework to contextual and stochastic settings
\cite{pacchiano2020model,marinov2021pareto,zhu2022pareto,zhu2022near,dann2024data}.
These approaches are inherently \emph{randomized} and target robustness: the
master performs nearly as well as the best base algorithm regardless of
which one that is. Our objective is different---we want a
\emph{deterministic} rule that exploits the complementarity between a good
early frozen policy and a strong asymptotic adaptive learner to
\emph{strictly outperform} both. Correspondingly, our analysis does not
bound a relative regret against the best base, but instead tracks how
early actions selected by one policy reshape the design matrix and
accelerate the statistical efficiency of another.

\paragraph{Warm starts and prior information in contextual bandits.}
Several works have investigated how prior information can be incorporated
into contextual bandit algorithms through initialization, offline datasets,
or pretrained predictors.
Such approaches can substantially improve short-horizon performance by
reducing uncertainty before online interaction begins
\cite{bastani2021mostly,zhang2019warm}.
Related directions in transfer learning and structured bandits further show
that leveraging information across tasks or user populations can accelerate
adaptation and reduce exploration requirements
\cite{lazaric2012transfer,gentile2014online}.
Meta-learning approaches similarly learn priors that enable faster
exploration in new environments
\cite{sharaf2019meta}.
Empirical work on neural-linear bandit architectures also demonstrates that
representation pretraining can significantly improve early decision quality
\cite{riquelme2018deep}.
Our analysis formalizes these intuitions in a linear bandit framework by
showing that informative early exploration induces a contraction of the
confidence ellipsoid, leading to a multiplicative reduction of the adaptive
regret term.

\paragraph{Geometry of information gain in linear bandits.}
A central theme in the analysis of linear bandit algorithms is that learning
efficiency is governed by the geometry of the accumulated design matrix.
Early work on stochastic linear optimization under bandit feedback showed that
regret depends explicitly on the growth of the covariance matrix associated
with the chosen actions and the resulting confidence ellipsoid
\cite{dani2008stochastic}.
Subsequent analyses of linearly parameterized bandits further clarified how the
eigenvalue structure of this matrix determines parameter identifiability and
sample complexity \cite{rusmevichientong2010linearly}.
Later work established sharp self-normalized confidence bounds and finite-time
regret guarantees expressed directly in terms of the design matrix and its
inverse \cite{abbasi2011improved}, while related results connected spectral
growth of the covariance matrix to optimal arm identification in linear
settings \cite{soare2014best}.
From an information-theoretic perspective, regret can also be related to
determinant growth and effective dimension of the covariance operator
\cite{srinivas2012information,valko2013finite}.
Our work extends this geometric viewpoint by showing that a fixed policy can
shape the spectrum of the design matrix during early rounds of interaction,
thereby improving conditioning of the confidence ellipsoid and accelerating
the learning rate of subsequent adaptive exploration.



\paragraph{Large language models for sequential decision making.}
Recent work has explored the use of large language models (LLMs) as policies
in contextual bandit environments, demonstrating that frozen pretrained
models can achieve strong short-horizon performance even without online
updates \cite{chen2024llmdecision}.
More broadly, LLMs exhibit strong in-context learning capabilities
\cite{brown2020language,xie2021explanation,garg2022can,akyurek2022learning},
and instruction tuning further improves their effectiveness in downstream
decision tasks \cite{ouyang2022training,chung2024scaling}.
Several recent approaches interpret sequential decision making itself as a
sequence-modeling problem and adapt transformer architectures to interactive
settings
\cite{chen2021decision,zheng2022online,reid2022can,
sun2023smart,raparthy2023generalization,lee2023supervised}.
Closest to our work is the approach of \cite{chen2024llmdecision}, who
combine an LLM policy with an adaptive contextual bandit learner using an
online model-selection master and empirically document the reward-
multiplication phenomenon on large-scale recommendation datasets. Our
contribution relative to that line is theoretical: we give a regret-based
explanation for why such combinations can beat both components, and replace
the randomized CORRAL-style master with a deterministic confidence-gated
rule whose routing decision directly exposes the geometric warm-start
mechanism.
A concurrent line of work \cite{huangcontextual} also uses LLM
uncertainty to gate exploration, but does so by calibrating an
embedding-based uncertainty estimate to adjust UCB-style exploration
bonuses, rather than by comparing self-normalized confidence radii of a
frozen predictor and an online learner; their analysis focuses on
regret improvement for a single calibrated agent, whereas we analyze
routing across multiple frozen experts and quantify a multiplicative
reduction in the post-handover adaptive regret term.
Additional evidence that LLMs can exhibit in-context reinforcement
learning behavior \cite{monea2024llms,krishnamurthy2024can} and that
LLM-derived priors can jump-start exploration
\cite{alamdari2024jumpstarting,abbasiyadkori2024believe} further
motivates the study of principled mechanisms for combining frozen
predictors with online learners.
Building on the reward-multiplication phenomenon observed in
\cite{chen2024llmdecision}, our work provides a regret-theoretic
explanation showing how early actions selected by a fixed policy reduce
the uncertainty radius of LinUCB and accelerate subsequent learning.
